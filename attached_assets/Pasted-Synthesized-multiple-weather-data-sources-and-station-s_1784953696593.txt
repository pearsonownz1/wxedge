Yes — and this is actually the best part: nearly all of it is free, public, and well-documented, because it's US government data. Let me walk through the settlement mechanics first (they matter more than you'd think), then the feeds.

What actually settles the contract. All weather market contracts settle on the final climate report issued by the National Weather Service, typically released the following morning — apps like AccuWeather, iOS Weather, or Google Weather may be useful for guidance but do not determine contract outcomes. Each city maps to one specific station: NYC settles on the NWS Daily Climate Report for Central Park (station KNYC), not LaGuardia, not JFK. That distinction alone is tradeable knowledge — Central Park's sensor sits in a tree-shaded enclosure and behaves differently from the airport stations most forecast products emphasize.

There's also a beautiful timing quirk: NWS Climate Reports use local standard time year-round, so during Daylight Saving Time the daily high is recorded between 1:00 AM and 12:59 AM local time the following day — not the calendar day people intuitively assume. Casual traders routinely get burned by this.

The data feeds you'd build on:

NWS API (api.weather.gov) — free, no API key, JSON. This is your workhorse. It gives you point forecasts, hourly forecasts, real-time station observations, and crucially the raw text products: the CLI (Daily Climate Report, the actual settlement document) and the DSM (Daily Summary Message, a preliminary version). You can pull observations for KNYC, KMDW, KAUS, etc. directly, typically updated hourly with special reports when conditions change fast.
Iowa Environmental Mesonet (mesonet.agron.iastate.edu) — the backtester's goldmine. IEM archives decades of ASOS/METAR observations for every settlement station, including one-minute ASOS data for many of them, plus an archive of historical CLI text products. This is how you build the historical dataset: for each station, every day for the past 10+ years, what did the forecast say at each horizon, and what did the CLI actually print?
HRRR model output — NOAA's high-resolution (3km) rapid-refresh model, new run every hour, free on AWS S3 (noaa-hrrr-bdp-pds bucket) and Google Cloud. The files are GRIB2 format, which is annoying raw, but the Python package Herbie makes pulling a 2m-temperature forecast for a specific lat/lon a few lines of code. This is your "better than the phone app" input.
NBM (National Blend of Models) — arguably even better than HRRR for this use case, because it's NOAA's own bias-corrected, probabilistic blend of dozens of models, published per-station. It gives you percentile distributions of the daily max, not just a point forecast. Also free on AWS.
RCC-ACIS (rcc-acis.org) — clean historical daily climate data with a simple JSON API. At least one existing scanner seeds its calibration with 90-day backtests from this database — which tells you two things: the pipeline is very buildable, and you're not the first to build it.

The intraday structural edge — this is the part worth underlining. The daily high is a running maximum, which is monotonic: once the station touches 87°F at 2pm, the settlement value can never come back below 87. So through the afternoon, entire brackets become mathematically dead or mathematically guaranteed while their prices sometimes lag. This is the exact same "partially locked-in settlement" logic as your friend's crypto idea — except the lock-in happens over hours instead of 60 seconds, which means a human with a Python script watching one-minute ASOS data can actually compete. The complication that keeps it interesting: settlement can be delayed when a high is inconsistent with 6-hr or 24-hr METAR highs, or when the final report comes in lower than the preliminary one — the hourly METAR rounds and occasionally misses brief spikes that the CLI catches from continuous sensor data, so "current observed high" and "final CLI high" can differ by a degree. That gap is precisely where your calibration work lives.

A realistic pipeline, in order: First, use IEM + ACIS to assemble 5-10 years of (forecast at horizon H, actual CLI high) pairs per station and measure each station's bias and error distribution by season — this costs nothing and tells you if the edge candidate exists before you write any trading logic. Second, log Kalshi's bracket prices (their public API provides order books) alongside your model's bracket probabilities for 60+ days without trading. Third, apply the same gate your friend's project used: when your model says 75%, does it hit 75%?

One honest tempering note, since the search results made it visible: there are already public scanners and even paid data products mapping these stations, so the pond has more fishermen than it did a couple of years ago. The durable version of the edge is probably station-specific homework (sensor quirks, the METAR-vs-CLI gap, the LST window, afternoon lock-in dynamics) rather than "I have a forecast and they don't." Still — as a learn-by-building project with capped downside and genuinely rich free data, it's about as good as this hobby gets.
